## Chapter 6 — Complex Measures

General convention: a complex measure is countably additive and takes finite complex values; a positive measure may take +∞. All set evaluations below are on the stated sigma algebra. “Unique in Lp” means unique modulo almost-everywhere equality.

| Node | Printed page | Kind | Statement and material subclauses |
|---|---:|---|---|
| 6.1 | 116–117 | Introduction / definitions | A measurable partition of E is a countable pairwise-disjoint family with union E. A complex measure μ satisfies μ(E)=Σ μ(Ej) for every such partition; each sum converges absolutely. Define total variation by `|μ|(E)=sup_partition Σ |μ(Ej)|`. This dominates `|μ(E)|`, is the least positive measure that does so (once 6.2 is established), and agrees with μ when μ is positive. The finite-mass assertion here is proved at 6.4. |
| 6.2 | 117–118 | Theorem | The total variation of any complex measure is a positive measure (in particular, countably additive). |
| 6.3 | 118 | Lemma | For complex numbers z₁,…,z_N there is S⊆{1,…,N} such that `|Σ_{k∈S} z_k| ≥ (1/π) Σ_{k=1}^N |z_k|`. Exact constant **1/π**, not a smaller easy constant. Visually checked on PDF p.131. |
| 6.4 | 118–119 | Theorem | Every complex measure has finite total variation mass: `|μ|(X)<∞`. |
| 6.5 | 119 | Definitions / assertion | Addition and complex scalar multiplication are setwise: `(μ+λ)(E)=μ(E)+λ(E)`, `(cμ)(E)=cμ(E)`. They preserve complex measures. These operations give a complex vector space, and `‖μ‖=|μ|(X)` is a norm. |
| 6.6 | 119 | Positive and negative variations | For a real (signed) measure μ, define `μ⁺=(|μ|+μ)/2`, `μ⁻=(|μ|−μ)/2`. Both are finite positive measures; `μ=μ⁺−μ⁻` and `|μ|=μ⁺+μ⁻`. This is the Jordan decomposition; minimality is deferred to 6.14's corollary. |
| 6.7 | 120 | Definitions | For positive μ, `λ≪μ` means μ(E)=0 ⇒ λ(E)=0. Concentration of λ on measurable A means λ(E)=λ(A∩E) for every E; equivalently λ(E)=0 for every E disjoint from A. Mutual singularity `λ₁⊥λ₂` means concentration on disjoint measurable sets. λ may be positive or complex. |
| 6.8 | 120–121 | Proposition (a)–(g) | (a) Concentration of λ on A implies concentration of |λ| on A. (b) λ₁⊥λ₂ ⇒ |λ₁|⊥|λ₂|. (c) λ₁⊥μ and λ₂⊥μ ⇒ λ₁+λ₂⊥μ. (d) λ₁≪μ and λ₂≪μ ⇒ λ₁+λ₂≪μ. (e) λ≪μ ⇒ |λ|≪μ. (f) λ₁≪μ and λ₂⊥μ ⇒ λ₁⊥λ₂. (g) λ≪μ and λ⊥μ ⇒ λ=0. μ is positive; λ-variables are measures of the applicable positive/complex kind. |
| 6.9 | 121 | Lemma | If μ is positive and sigma finite on X, there is measurable integrable w with **0<w(x)<1 for every x**. The associated finite measure wμ has precisely μ's null sets. |
| 6.10 | 121–123 | Lebesgue–Radon–Nikodym theorem (a),(b) | For positive sigma-finite μ and complex λ: (a) a unique pair of complex measures λ_a,λ_s satisfies `λ=λ_a+λ_s`, `λ_a≪μ`, `λ_s⊥μ`; if λ is positive finite then so are both parts. (b) there is a unique h∈L¹(μ) with `λ_a(E)=∫_E h dμ` for every measurable E. h is the Radon–Nikodym derivative. Subsequent discussion extends positive lambda to sigma finite with locally integrable h, and explains failure without sigma finiteness; do not silently weaken the numbered finite-complex theorem. |
| 6.11 | 124 | Theorem (a),(b) | For positive μ and complex λ, absolute continuity λ≪μ is equivalent to: for every ε>0 there is δ>0 such that μ(E)<δ implies `|λ(E)|<ε` for every measurable E. No sigma-finiteness hypothesis. The text explicitly warns that the equivalence fails for unbounded positive λ. |
| 6.12 | 124–125 | Theorem | Every complex μ admits a **measurable** h with **|h(x)|=1 at every x**, and `dμ=h d|μ|`. An a.e.-unit representative alone is weaker than the printed statement; choose a unit value on the exceptional null set. |
| 6.13 | 125 | Theorem | If μ is positive, g∈L¹(μ), and `λ(E)=∫_E g dμ`, then `|λ|(E)=∫_E |g| dμ` for all measurable E. No sigma-finiteness assumption. |
| 6.14 | 125–126 | Hahn decomposition theorem + unnumbered corollary | For real μ, there are measurable A,B partitioning X with `μ⁺(E)=μ(A∩E)` and `μ⁻(E)=−μ(B∩E)` for all E. Thus subsets of A have nonnegative μ and subsets of B nonpositive μ. **Corollary:** if `μ=λ₁−λ₂` with positive measures λ₁,λ₂, then `μ⁺≤λ₁` and `μ⁻≤λ₂`. |
| 6.15 | 126–127 | Setup / assertions | For positive μ, 1≤p≤∞ and conjugate q, each g∈Lq defines `Φ_g(f)=∫_X fg dμ` on Lp, a bounded **complex linear** functional with `‖Φ_g‖≤‖g‖q`. At p=∞ representation by L¹ fails in general (the cited construction is an out-of-scope exercise). It announces full representation for 1<p<∞ and for p=1 under sigma finiteness, proved in 6.16. |
| 6.16 | 127–129 | Theorem | For 1≤p<∞, positive sigma-finite μ, and bounded complex linear Φ on Lp(μ), there is a unique g∈Lq(μ) with `Φ(f)=∫ fg dμ` for every f∈Lp, and **norm equality** `‖Φ‖=‖g‖q`. The product is fg, not f·conj(g); using a Hilbert-space convention needs an explicit conjugation bridge. |
| 6.17 | 129 | Remark | The p=q=2 case is Hilbert-space representation; it was used to prove Radon–Nikodym, which in turn proved general Lp dual representation. The essential mathematical assertion to reference is the p=2 specialization, together with the dependency explanation; this is a numbered node even though no additional independent theorem is stated. |
| 6.18 | 129–130 | Setup / definitions / assertions | For locally compact Hausdorff X, a sup-norm bounded linear functional on Cc(X) extends uniquely to C₀(X). Define complex integration by `∫f dμ=∫fh d|μ|` for a unit polar density h; indicator integral equals μ(E), and bounded measurable functions satisfy additivity in μ. A complex Borel measure is regular when its total variation is regular. Integration against any complex Borel measure is a bounded linear functional on C₀(X), with norm at most |μ|(X). |
| 6.19 | 130–132 | Riesz representation theorem | On **any locally compact Hausdorff X**, every bounded complex linear functional on C₀(X) has a **unique regular complex Borel measure** μ representing it on every f∈C₀(X), with **exact norm** `‖Φ‖=|μ|(X)`. Do not add second countability, sigma compactness, compactness, or replace complex measures by positive measures without declaring a genuine gap. |

## Chapter 7 — Differentiation

Convention: m is Lebesgue measure on real Euclidean R^k (k≥1), B(x,r) is the open Euclidean ball. Shrinking-radius limits are through positive radii. Lebesgue-measurable versus Borel hypotheses are material, especially in 7.26.

| Node | Printed page | Kind | Statement and material subclauses |
|---|---:|---|---|
| 7.1 | 135–136 | Theorem (a),(b) | For a complex Borel measure μ on R, define `f(x)=μ((−∞,x))`. For x∈R and A∈C, `f'(x)=A` is equivalent to: for every ε>0 there is δ>0 such that `|μ(I)/m(I)−A|<ε` for **every open interval I containing x** of length less than δ. Centered intervals alone do not state the full result. |
| 7.2 | 136 | Definitions / assertion | Define `Q_r μ(x)=μ(B(x,r))/m(B(x,r))`; Dμ(x) is its r→0 limit where it exists. For positive μ, `Mμ(x)=sup_{r>0} Q_rμ(x)`; for complex μ set Mμ=M|μ|. Maximal functions are lower semicontinuous and therefore measurable. |
| 7.3 | 137 | Covering lemma (a)–(c) | For a finite family of balls with union W, choose an index subset S so that (a) selected balls are pairwise disjoint; (b) their **triple-radius** balls cover W; (c) `m(W)≤3^k Σ_{i∈S}m(B_i)`. Constant 3^k and finite nature are explicit. |
| 7.4 | 137 | Maximal theorem | For a complex Borel measure μ and λ>0, `m{x:Mμ(x)>λ}≤3^k λ⁻¹ ‖μ‖`, where `‖μ‖=|μ|(R^k)`. |
| 7.5 | 138 | Weak L¹ / definitions and assertions | Integrable f satisfies `m{|f|>λ}≤λ⁻¹‖f‖₁`. Define weak L¹ by boundedness of `λ·m{|f|>λ}` on λ>0; L¹ is included strictly (example 1/x on (0,1)). | 7.6 | 138 | Lebesgue points | A point x is a Lebesgue point of an integrable function f when ball averages of `|f(y)−f(x)|` tend to zero. Continuity at x is sufficient. This definition refers to an actual representative f, not just an L¹ equivalence class. |

| 7.9 | 140 | Nicely shrinking sets | A sequence of Borel sets E_i shrinks nicely to x when some α>0 and radii r_i→0 satisfy `E_i⊆B(x,r_i)` and `m(E_i)≥α m(B(x,r_i))` for all i. x need not lie in E_i or its closure. | 7.11 | 141 | Theorem | For f∈L¹(R), `F(x)=∫_{(−∞,x)}f dm` has derivative f(x) at every Lebesgue point of f, hence a.e. |

| 7.13 | 142 | Theorem | Given nicely shrinking E_i(x), if complex Borel μ⊥m then `μ(E_i(x))/m(E_i(x))→0` m-a.e.; in particular Dμ=0 m-a.e. |
| 7.14 | 143 | Theorem | For arbitrary complex Borel μ and nicely shrinking E_i(x), write its Lebesgue decomposition `dμ=f dm+dμ_s`. Then `μ(E_i(x))/m(E_i(x))→f(x)` m-a.e. In particular `μ⊥m` iff `Dμ=0` m-a.e. |

| 7.20 | 148 | Theorem | A complex AC f on [a,b] is differentiable a.e., its derivative is L¹, and `f(x)−f(a)=∫_a^x f'` for every x∈[a,b]. |
| 7.21 | 149–150 | Theorem | If real f:[a,b]→R is differentiable **at every point** and f'∈L¹([a,b]), then the FTC identity holds for every x∈[a,b]. At the interval endpoints use the natural one-sided/domain derivative interpretation. |
